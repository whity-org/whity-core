2.3Aims of the project
In plain language. No more than 300 words.

This study asks whether first-year students who sleep less than six hours a night before an exam score lower than those who sleep seven or more, once study time, caffeine and commuting time are taken into account.

We will compare self-reported sleep from a one-week diary with exam marks released by the registry, with the students’ written consent. The findings will inform the Deanship of Student Affairs’ guidance on exam timetabling and the health centre’s sleep workshops.

Secondary aims: to describe how sleep changes across the exam week, and whether students who live more than an hour from campus are more affected.

2.4Background and justification
Why this research is needed. No more than 500 words.

Short sleep is common among university students and is linked in many studies to poorer attention and memory. Most of that research comes from North America and Europe, where students commute less and timetables differ. Local evidence is thin: two small surveys at Jordanian universities reported average sleep under six hours during exams but did not link it to marks.

Students at Example University sit up to four exams in five days during the first-semester exam week. Many travel from Zarqa, Salt or Madaba by bus, adding up to three hours a day. The registry reports that first-year failure rates rise in courses whose exams fall early in the morning.

Diary methods are cheap and accepted by students; a one-week diary has been validated against wrist-worn trackers in several student populations, with acceptable agreement for total sleep time. Linking diaries to registry marks avoids self-reported grades, a known source of bias.

The study also responds to a request from the university health centre, which runs sleep workshops each semester but has no figures on how many first-year students sleep too little during exams, or whether those who attend the workshops do better. Our diary data will give the centre a baseline to plan the next round of workshops and to measure them against.

Exam timetables are set by the registry two months ahead, and the Deanship of Student Affairs has asked for evidence before it changes the order or start times of first-year exams. A clear local answer, even a negative one, would let both offices decide on data rather than complaints.

2.5Methodology
No more than 500 words.

A prospective cohort of first-year students in four faculties. Volunteers keep a one-page sleep diary each morning of exam week (Arabic or English) and, at the end, consent to the registry releasing their marks for the four exams of that week. No intervention is given.

Analysis: a mixed model of exam mark on sleep the night before, with student as a random effect, adjusting for study hours, caffeine and commuting time.
